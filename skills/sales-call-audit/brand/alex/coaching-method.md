# Coaching method — source-backed decision cards

Load this with the generic skill and voice profile. These are operational interpretations, not quotations, universal conversion laws, or guarantees. H1 refers to the supplied ACQ Closer Handbook; V1/V2 resolve in source-profile.md. Current approved offer facts take precedence over historical examples. Use the card that fits the observed decision; do not audit every card on every call.

## 1. Separate opportunity creation from conversion
**Source:** H1 On-Going Schedule; Hunt Mode / Kill Mode.
Determine whether the call's job was to find the buyer, qualify, book a conversation, close, or follow up. Check success against that job. A useful booked appointment can be a successful setter call even without a purchase. An unanswered dial provides no evidence about a rep's discovery skill.

## 2. Use prior context before repeating discovery
**Source:** H1 Call Notes; Script Bank introduction.
Read available setter notes and identify what the buyer actually confirmed on this call. Prior education, an inbound application, or a previous meeting can change which questions are necessary. The handbook explicitly explains why some scripts abbreviate discovery. Missing prior records should be a coverage limit, not automatic evidence the rep skipped qualification.

## 3. Frame the call in few words
**Source:** H1 Introduction; Outbound Set and Closing Script.
Check that the rep established who is calling, the relevant reason, disclosure required by the applicable process, available time, and agenda. If the buyer is confused about the caller or cannot hear, repair that before more discovery. Replace a long introduction with one clear sentence and a time check. Do not present the handbook's legal assertions as universal law.

## 4. Establish current state and desired state
**Source:** H1 Discovery steps 1–2; V1 3507–3609s; V2 9882–10032s.
Find what the buyer has now and what they want to change. Broad interest in information calls for a useful clarifying question. If the buyer already gave a specific goal, use it; don't ask them to manufacture a different one. Keep measured business facts distinct from estimates.

## 5. Ask for an example when the answer is vague
**Source:** H1 Discovery steps 3–5; V2 10034–10096s.
Ask what blocks the goal, what has been tried, and for a specific example when the answer is vague, confusing, or incomplete. A concrete example should change the next question. Asking for more pain after the need is already clear can waste time and make the conversation repetitive.

## 6. Recap, label, confirm
**Source:** H1 Discovery steps 6–8.
Restate the buyer's problem in their language, map it to a relevant solution category, and ask whether the recap is right. A correction supersedes the rep's earlier assumption. The next question must use the corrected facts. A label like “operations” is a routing aid; it does not replace the concrete problem.

## 7. Know when discovery is sufficient
**Source:** H1 Discovery step 9 and Final Recap.
Discovery can advance when the rep has enough confirmed, relevant problems to explain how the offer helps. Repeat only to resolve a material gap. Do not invent extra problems or ask every question merely to finish a list. Summarize the confirmed problems before transitioning.

## 8. Preserve what worked in earlier attempts
**Source:** V1 3759–3820s; V2 10034–10096s.
Learn what the previous solution provided, what failed, and what remains unresolved. Identify a missing element only when supported. A buyer taking a call is not proof their existing provider is incompetent. Avoid insulting the buyer's previous decision or inventing a reason it failed.

## 9. Make the offer from discovery notes
**Source:** H1 Offer and solution stack.
Use buyer problem → actual capability → credible assurance → buyer benefit → confirmation. Keep the buyer problem visible through the explanation. If the proposed feature does not address it, the coach should say so. Assurance needs to match the concern: relevant experience, clear scope, or verified proof, not an unrelated size or status claim. The source's short offer target is a concision guide, not a universal clock-based failure rule.

## 10. Explain what changes for the buyer
**Source:** V2 10154–10418s.
Replace unnecessary modules, jargon, and process detail with what the buyer can do or obtain from the real service. Use a short analogy only when it clarifies an actual mechanism. Do not hide the work required, delivery limits, or material terms. A story illustrating a concept must not masquerade as a real customer result.

## 11. Make the ask and leave room for an answer
**Source:** H1 Offer; Tone; Closing Script.
Identify the stage-appropriate ask. If the rep immediately adds more pitch before the buyer can respond, recommend the exact ask followed by a pause. Without audio or reliable speaker timing, do not claim a particular silence length or vocal problem. Do not treat lack of payment as failure when the goal was an appointment.

## 12. Address known barriers early
**Source:** V2 6235–6418s.
If the buyer has already raised attendance, implementation, authority, or fit constraints, explore them before treating the close as a surprise objection. The course distinguishes obstacles before price from objections afterward; H1 uses five practical buying-objection families. These are complementary lenses, not interchangeable quoted frameworks.

## 13. Confirm value and isolate the concern
**Source:** H1 Looping: Confirming Value, Triage; Looping Script steps 1–2.
A generic hesitation calls for finding out whether the buyer believes the offer helps and what remains unresolved. If the buyer has already identified a concrete concern, acknowledge it directly; don't act as though it was never said. Confirm the concern and ask whether another issue also matters before selecting a response. Do not assume certainty from a polite “yeah.”

## 14. Add information without making the buyer wrong
**Source:** H1 Looping, Gotcha Moment; V1 5507–5537s.
Address the content of the concern. Explain the missing information or a different way to evaluate it, then check the buyer's response and invite the appropriate next step. Don't shame the buyer, attack their priorities, or imply their assets prove they can afford the offer. Agreement is useful only if the rep then addresses the actual issue.

## 15. Money needs diagnosis
**Source:** H1 Money Objections and Looping Script.
Distinguish affordability, cash timing, expected value, and risk of another failed purchase. Ask only financial questions relevant to fit, with permission. Use the buyer's real numbers; don't invent ROI or lost revenue. If cash is genuinely unavailable, use an appropriate follow-up or disqualify. Do not prescribe debt or a private funding mechanism merely to force a close.

## 16. Time needs a concrete constraint
**Source:** H1 Timing Objections and Looping Script.
Find whether the issue is calendar dates, time to implement, operational coverage, or priority. Explain a time-saving feature only if it exists and applies. The claim that all time objections are priority objections is a training heuristic, not a license to dismiss caregiving, health, staffing, or other real constraints. Do not promise flexible dates unless the current policy permits them.

## 17. Map the actual decision process
**Source:** H1 Decision-Maker and BAMFAM; V2 6390–6418s.
Establish whether the other person approves spending, shares the decision, or implements the purchase. Ask what they would need to evaluate. For a joint business decision, an appropriate next step may be a meeting with that stakeholder. Do not coach the rep to misrepresent authority, hide a purchase, or invent a cancellation guarantee.

## 18. Separate a question from a fit failure
**Source:** H1 Preference Objections; prior attempts and offer mapping.
When the buyer needs a different delivery model, ask what must be included. Explain the actual model and check it against the requirement. If the offer cannot meet it, a clean no can be good selling. If the buyer reports conflicting information, verify the facts before persuading. A longer feature explanation does not resolve a contradiction by itself.

## 19. Give a stalled decision a specific subject
**Source:** H1 Stall Objections; V2 10609–10717s.
Ask what the buyer wants to think through or fears will happen. Work through that uncertainty together if welcome. Don't treat every request for time as a disguised yes or use founder anecdotes to override a buyer's decision process. Use a follow-up when more information or another participant is genuinely needed.

## 20. Finish the next step while still on the call
**Source:** H1 BAMFAM and Script Bank.
When the buyer wants to continue, agree the purpose, participants, preparation, day/time/timezone, and invite receipt where observable. “I'll send something” is not an appointment. If the buyer clearly declines further contact, respect it. The handbook's pipeline also explicitly distinguishes opt-outs from reachable prospects.

## 21. Reinforce the purchase through onboarding
**Source:** V2 9937–9986s and 10717–10755s; H1 Closing/Referral scripts.
After a purchase, confirm what happened and what comes next. Distinguish a promise, submission, reported receipt, partial payment, and independently verified payment. Explain next actions and ownership. Ask for referrals at a suitable point without making onboarding conditional on an introduction.

## 22. Keep the prospect visible during script delivery
**Source:** H1 Breathe the Script; V2 6000–6138s.
Preparation frees attention to listen. If the rep mechanically repeats a question already answered, coach the listening/branching decision rather than adding another memorized line. A successful sale does not prove every incidental phrase in it was useful. Avoid expanding scripts with behaviors supported only by one coincidental win.

## 23. Coach one behavior with immediate practice
**Source:** H1 Onboarding/On-Going Training; V1 1300–1550s; V2 6132–6218s.
Choose the highest-leverage observable issue. Frame what is being practiced and why, model it, let the rep attempt it, give prompt feedback on that one behavior, repeat the correct version, then recap. After successful repetition, vary the buyer's answer. This is skill practice; passing it does not validate a conversion claim.

## 24. Keep delivery assessment within the evidence
**Source:** H1 Tone; V2 10764–10880s.
The source separates pace, volume, clarity, pitch, and pauses. Review audible properties only with audio. Use a transcript to assess wording and, where supported, turns and timing. The training's pace and conversion statistics are not validated universal thresholds. A suggested `[wait]` is an instruction for future delivery, not proof the original rep failed to pause.

## 25. Improve across calls without overgeneralizing
**Source:** H1 Career Path, training schedules, worst-call review, Hunt Mode.
Repeatable behavior matters more than one outcome. Track the chosen behavior across comparable calls and retain positive and negative examples. Don't rank reps from one call, use old internal productivity targets as universal benchmarks, or turn the source's motivational rhetoric into coercive customer language. Coaching a single uploaded call does not authorize outreach, CRM changes, or contact scheduling.
